# Abstract {#abstract .list-paragraph}

Achieving an appropriate trade-off between biological utility and
patient privacy remains a key challenge for secure data sharing when
applying transcriptomic clinical datasets to artificial intelligence in
precision oncology. Here, we introduce the first disease-agnostic
benchmarking study tailored to high-dimensional clinical transcriptomic
cancer data, comparing synthetic data generation methods across three
cancer types. The proposed pipeline combines standardized preprocessing
with multidimensional evaluation, prioritizing downstream biological
validation alongside statistical fidelity and attack-based privacy
assessment. Results indicate that no single method dominated all
dimensions, with Gaussian Copula achieving the most balanced
performance, and demonstrate that metric-based similarity alone is
insufficient to ensure preservation of higher-order molecular
dependencies. Synthetic data consistently reproduced signal
directionality but with attenuated effect sizes, supporting their use
for hypothesis generation. Collectively, this framework provides a
reproducible decision-support tool for method selection and promotes
biologically informed, privacy-aware adoption of synthetic data in
precision oncology.

# Introduction

High throughput omics technologies facilitate researchers to investigate
cancer mechanisms at the molecular level, so that they can understand
how tumors initiate, progresses and responds to treatments.^1^ For
example, transcriptomics makes it possible to simultaneously quantify
the expression at the RNA level across thousands of genes. Even better,
when integrated with clinical data, these approaches can characterise
the dynamical genome-wide alterations during disease progression and
allow for biomarker discovery, patient stratification and outcome
prediction.^2,3,4^ Such applications are further empowered by artificial
intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML), where multi-omics data
could be processed, integrated to develop reliable predictive
or decision-making models.^5^ This makes data-driven solutions as an
essential component of digital medicine and precision oncology.

Realizing the full potential of data-driven precision oncology
increasingly depends on the ability to share and integrate large-scale,
diverse datasets across institutions. Data sharing reduces redundant
data production efforts, supports cross-institutional collaborations,
and improves the external validation of AI models. However, cancer omics
data are restricted and sensitive, as these measurements represent not
only molecular levels but also identifiable clinical phenotypes.^6^
Therefore, the implementation of regulatory framework such as the
General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the European Union^7^ and
the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)^8^ in
the United States ensures that health data sharing adheres to strict
protocols to safeguard patient privacy. There are two common
privacy-preserving methods: pseudonymization and anonymization. While
pseudonymization removes explicit identifiers, it still carries a
considerable risk of identification. On the other hand, anonymization
does better by employing techniques to mask and transform data but it
can harm utility once privacy reaches a certain level. In the worst
case, excessive anonymization can disrupt multivariate relationships,
rendering the data unsuitable for downstream analytical tasks.^9^

Recently, synthetic data has emerged as a promising solution for the
data-sharing problem. Technically, synthetic data are artificial patient
records that mimic the statistical properties of real data while
reducing privacy risk.^9^ As recent works indicate, synthetic data can
be used in multiple scenarios from estimating the effectiveness
of health care interventions and screening policies to promote machine
learning via pre-training models or improve predictions of infectious
disease outbreaks.^10^ As a result, this demand has promoted the rapid
development of sophisticated data synthesis methods known as deep
learning frameworks, such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)^11^
and Variational Autoencoders (VAEs),^12^ and statistical techniques,
such as copula models.^13^

Despite its application within different domains, it remains unclear how
reliable synthetic data is for multi-omics cancer research. These data
modalities have their own specific challenges. The source of original
data used to train algorithms is not frequently abundant, especially
when that data comes from clinical cancer studies. These studies often
involve few patients, particularly in clinical trials or for rare cancer
types. This means there are few samples with both clinical variables and
transcriptomic or genomic data. Transcriptomic profiles, in particular,
pose a high-dimensional challenge, typically comprising tens of
thousands of genes relative to a limited sample size. When
transcriptomic measurements are integrated with clinical variables. The
resulting datasets become heterogeneous, encompassing mixed data types
such as continuous gene expression values, categorical variables (e.g.,
treatment groups), and ordinal clinical features (e.g., disease stage).
Moreover, biologically meaningful relationships between gene expression
patterns and clinical outcomes are often non-linear and high-order,
making them difficult to capture with simplistic generative assumptions.
Last but not least, missing data resulting from technical problems,
studies' design, or clinical practice also constitutes an additional
limitation. Together, these factors make it challenging to generate
synthetic clinical transcriptomics data. Naive use of generic methods
may risk compromising of biological fidelity or exposing sensitive
patient-level information.

Although studies on synthetic data are increasing, to the best of our
knowledge, none systematically address these challenges for clinical
transcriptomic data, leaving a critical void to be filled. First,
unified benchmarking studies of these data modalities are needed to
contrast methods or to derive general insights about such data.
Second, the preprocessing procedures, such as clean-up, normalization,
missing values imputation and data combination, before the training of
the models, have a great influence on the quality of the synthetic data,
but they are sometimes not consistently executed or sufficiently
disclosed. Third, many evaluation methods are based on
statistical measures, such as marginal distributions and bivariate
correlations, which are insufficient to ascertain that biologically or
clinically relevant signals have been retained. Such biological analyses
as differential gene expression, pathway enrichment, immune cell
deconvolution or survival analysis are infrequently applied or omitted
altogether. Fourth, there is no consensus on privacy metrics and
evaluations and many studies employ distance-based metrics that may not
properly represent realistic privacy risk, obscuring the interpretation
of privacy-utility trade-off.^9^ Lastly, none of the consideration of
computational cost and scalability, which are important factors for
translation into the clinical environment, were considered. Taken
together, these limitations make it challenging to reliably evaluate
synthetic data for clinical multi-omics applications, where either
biological or privacy are of utmost importance.

To address these gaps, we propose a general, disease-agnostic
benchmarking framework to systematically evaluate synthetic data
generation (SDG) methods for clinical transcriptomic cancer data. Our
framework integrates standardized preprocessing and method-aware
adaptations tailored for high-dimensional transcriptomics settings,
together with multi-dimensional quality assessment involving statistical
fidelity (broad utility), biological signal preservation (narrow
utility), and rivacy risk evaluation aligned with regulatory principles.
Computational cost is also reported to shed light on the practicality
and scalability of SDG methods in real-world applications.

In conclusion, this study establishes a systematic and biologically
grounded benchmarking framework for synthetic clinical transcriptomic
data. Researchers can leverage our proposed framework as a decision
support tool to select SDG methods aligned with the statistical,
biological, and privacy characteristics of their own datasets.
Furthermore, the standardized and extensible pipeline offers a reusable
foundation for method developers to systematically test, refine, and
design next-generation synthetic data algorithms tailored to
high-dimensional clinical transcriptomic settings. In doing so, this
work promotes more transparent, biologically meaningful, and
privacy-conscious adoption of synthetic data in precision oncology.

# Results

## The overview of the benchmarking protocol

Figure 1 summarizes the benchmarking setup for clinical transcriptomic
synthetic data generation. As illustrate in Figure 1a, we benchmarked
the methods using three cancer cohorts: CheckMate (CM) 009, 010, 025 for
clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) from Braun et al.,^3^ melanoma
from Liu et al.,^14^ and Stand Up To Cance-Mark Foundation (SU2C-MARK)
for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) from Ravi et al.,^15^ all treated
with immune checkpoint blockade. These datasets include clinical
variables such as age, sex, and survival outcomes, along with bulk gene
expression profiles. Table 1 summarizes the descriptive statistics of
samples containing both clinical information and bulk transcriptomic
data across three cancer types. We compared five SDG methods:
conditional tabular generative adversarial networks (CTGAN),^16^ tabular
variational autoencoders (TVAE),^16^ Gaussian Copula,^13^ Synthpop^17^,
and Avatars.^18^ The Avatars method was evaluated under two
configurations, with k (number of nearest neighbors) set to 5 and 10
(Avatars K5 and Avatars K10). Prior to data synthesis, all original
datasets were processed using a standardized pipeline to ensure the
methods comparable without bias (illustrated in Figure 1c; detailed in
Methods section). Then, each processed dataset was employed to train
every SDG model with 5 independent random seeds, resulting in 90
synthetic datasets for 6 SDG algorithms and three cancer types.

Synthetic data quality was subsequently evaluated based on three primary
pillars: broad utility, narrow utility, and privacy risk. Broad utility
validated the degree to which the synthetic data preserved the
statistical properties of the original dataset by comparing the
distribution of individual attributes (univariate similarity) and their
inter-variable relationships (bivariate similarity). Narrow utility
evaluated whether essential biological signals were preserved within the
synthetic data. We designed narrow utility validation frameworks
tailored for transcriptomic downstream tasks, including differential
gene expression (DGE),^19^ gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA),^20^
single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA),^21^ cell type deconvolution,^22^ and
survival analysis. Specifically, for each task, we re-implemented the
analytical workflows described in the original studies to determine
whether the established biomarkers and biological insights could be
independently rediscovered from synthetic data. Furthermore, privacy
risk was assessed according to the three criteria: singling out,
linkability, and inference, proposed by the European Data Protection
Board (EDPB) to ensure clear interpretability of data anonymity.^23^
Computational cost was also reported alongside the quality to evaluate
the practical feasibility and scalability. Finally, we calculated a
rank-derived meta-score for each SDG approach across all three cancer
cohorts to obtain an overall ranking.^24^ The evaluation workflow is
illustrated in Figure 1d and detailed further in the Methods section.

### Univariate similarity

The univariate similarity scores reflect how well each SDG method
preserves the marginal distribution of individual attributes. To
calculate to score, we used the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) statistic on the
numerical features and the Total variation distance (TVD) on the
categorical features. As shown in Table 2, Synthpop consistently
outperformed other methods across all three cohorts, achieving mean
scores of replicates above 0.92 (e.g., 0.952 ± 0.001 for ccRCC). In
contrast, TVAE exhibited the lowest values and highest variability,
particularly, within the ccRCC cohort (0.627 ± 0.027).

When we look closer at the distribution of these scores, a clear divide
emerges between data types. Most methods handled clinical attributes
well (Figure 2a), with both Synthpop and Gaussian Copula maintaining
tight distributions and high fidelity. However, the high-dimensional
nature of transcriptomic data proved much more difficult to replicate
(Figure 2b). While deep learning methods such as CTGAN and TVAE showed
inconsistent performance across the vast array of gene expression
profiles, Synthpop remained highly stable, maintaining a consistent mean
score above 0.90 in all three cancers. The results were similar in other
replicates as reported in Figure S1 in the Supplementary Data. Finally,
we performed Bayesian analysis on the results of 5 replicates to infer
the "better probability" among the methods. As illustrated in the
heatmaps in Figure 2c and Table S1-S3 in the Supplemetary Data, Synthpop
achieves close to 100% probability of superior all other methods for all
tested oncology cohorts.

### Bivariate Similarity

While univariate metrics confirm that marginal distributions are
preserved, they do not guarantee that the intricate \"co-dependence\"
between features is captured. Therefore, we evaluated the bivariate
similarity between original and synthetic data. Specifically, we
compared Spearman's rank correlation for numerical feature pairs and
Cramér's V for categorical associations and aggregated them into an
overall bivariate score. The calculation procedure is described in the
Methods section. As summarized in Table 2, the bivariate results
indicate a substantial shift in method performance. Synthpop, which led
in univariate validation, was surpassed by Avatars K5 and Gaussian
Copula. Avatars K5 achieved the best performance for ccRCC (0.995 ±
0.001) and NSCLC (0.941 ± 0.003) datasets, whereas Gaussian Copula
obtained the best fidelity in the Melanoma dataset (0.939 ± 0.001).
Additionally, throughout all three cancer datasets, pairwise Bayesian
comparison heatmaps indicate that Gaussian Copula and Avatars (K5/K10,
depending on cohort) consistently demonstrate the highest probabilities
of outperforming other methods in preserving bivariate relationship
(Figure 3a and Table S4-S6).

A deeper examination of these scores' distribution reveals a notable
disparity between data types. At first glance, all SDG method appeared
to perform exceptionally well, with mean bivariate scores surpassing
0.90. However, this is largely driven by the abundance of weakly or
non-correlated feature pairs. These low correlation noise pairs
naturally yield elevated bivariate similarity scores, thereby concealing
the genuine performance disparities among the algorithms. Therefore, we
extracted and visualized only the scores of feature pairs with a
correlation coefficient higher than 0.5 (Figure 3b and Figure S2). Under
this lens, synthetic datasets exhibited a marked decline in bivariate
similarity score, varying by association type. For clinical-clinical
associations, Avatars (K5 and K10) consistently achieved the highest
mean scores across all three cohorts, with narrower distributions than
other methods. For transcriptomic-transcriptomic associations, Avatars
(K5/K10), Gaussian Copula, and Synthpop achieved high mean scores in the
Melanoma and NSCLC datasets. In the ccRCC cohort, which is the most
complex dataset (\>40,000 features), the performance of Avatars and
Gaussian Copula declined relative to the more stable performance of
Synthpop. Notably, all SDG methods showed substantial deterioration when
preserving clinical-transcriptomic associations.

To determine whether these statistical correlations corresponded to
biologically meaningful structures, we performed hierarchical clustering
of correlation matrices derived from the Melanoma dataset (Figure 3c and
Figure S3), following Liu et al. The original data revealed a highly
structured architecture, including a prominent immune-related cluster
with distinct Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) class I and MHC
class II subclusters, a separate cluster of mutation and neoantigen load
features, and a strong negative correlation between tumor purity and the
immune cluster, where reduced purity served as a proxy for increased
immune infiltration. Significantly, the Avatars (K5/K10) and Gaussian
Copula successfully recapitulated these biologically coherent blocks. In
contrast, the matrices produced by CTGAN and TVAE appeared noisy or
excessively smoothed, failing to maintain the integrity of these
critical biological relationships. Together, these results provide a
different coverage on the utility of data, showing that a good
reproduction of marginal distributions does not necessarily ensures the
preservation of more complex relationships between features. This
discrepancy emphasizes the need to move beyond univariate measures to
assess more profound structural soundness via bivariate analysis and,
ultimately, narrow utility evaluations.

## Narrow utility

meaningful signals within downstream transcriptomic analyses.
Specifically, we evaluated the concordance between original and
synthetic datasets across bioinformatic relevant tasks: DGE, GSEA,
ssGSEA, cell type deconvolution, survival analysis, and machine
learning-based modeling. Task-specific metrics are summarized in Table 3
and detailed calculating procedure of each task is described in the
Methods section. Furthermore, we extracted established biomarkers and
biological insights from the original studies and re-implemented them on
the synthetic datasets to verify their capacity to rediscover critical
biological information. The results are categorized into specific
utility sections according to the analytical technique and the
biological context.

### Differential gene expression analysis (DGE)

To validate the performance of synthetic data in preserving differential
expression signals, we first compared the synthetic datasets based on
our developed metric called Gene Concordance Score (GCS). This metric
provides a single integrated measure that quantifies the weighted
proportion of synthetic genes concordant with the original data across
two key dimensions: regulation direction (up- or down-regulation) and
level of statistical significance. By jointly accounting for these two
aspects, GCS enables a one-shot assessment of whether synthetic data
preserve not only the directionality of gene regulation but also their
significance patterns. Higher GCS values indicate a larger proportion of
synthetic genes that faithfully preserve both the directionality and the
degree of statistical significance observed in the original pathways. We
described clearly the calculation protocol of this metric in the Methods
section. As summarized in Table 3, the Gaussian Copula model
consistently achieved the highest PCS values across all cohorts.
Bayesian estimation over five replicates showed that the Gaussian Copula
achieved superior probabilities (exceeding 70%) compared to alternative
methods (Figure 4a and Table S7-S9). Additionally, we also computed
Spearman's rank correlation to assess the monotonic concordances of
gene-wise $\log_{2}{fold - change}$($\log_{2}{FC}$) values between
original and synthetic datasets (Figure S7 and Table S10). Gaussian
Copula also the achieved the highest correlations across three cancer
types. The correlation values are modest among all SDG methods
especially on ccRCC datasets, which contains over 40,000 gene expression
profiles. Of note, there is one replicate of generated by Gaussian
Copula reached a Spearman correlation of 0.63.

To further validate biological utility, we applied the original
DGE-based analyses to synthetic datasets to ensure they recapitulated
the same biological signals found in the original data. For instance, in
ccRCC dataset, Braun et al. identified that PBRM1 alterations are
associated with increased angiogenesis gene expression. Both Avatars
(K5/K10) and Gaussian Copula successfully reproduced this clinical
signal (Figure 4b, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, $P < 0.01$). In the context
of Melanoma, Liu et al. observed higher expression of 13 MHC class
II-associated HLA genes in treatment responders, with four genes
(HLA-DMA, HLA-DMB, HLA-DOA, and HLA-DOB) reaching statistical
significance (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, $P < 0.05$). While Avatars
(K5/K10), Gaussian Copula, Synthpop, and TVAE successfully captured
responder upregulation, the statistical significance was notably
attenuated in the Avatars K10 and Gaussian Copula cohorts. In the
Avatars K10 synthetic data, only HLA-DOA and HLA-DOB maintained
significance, whereas all genes in the Gaussian Copula failed to meet
the 5% threshold. Conversely, Avatars K5 and TVAE exhibited an elevation
in false-positive gene identifications. Lastly, regarding the NSCLC
cohort, the authors reported a convergence of differentially expressed
genes (PSME1, PSME2, PSMB8, PSMB9, and PSMB10) within the
immunoproteasome system, which was significantly enriched in responders
relative to canonical Hallmark IFN-$\gamma$ targets as well as a
comprehensive list of proteasome components (GOCC_PROTEASOME_COMPLEX)
(Wilcoxon rank-sum test, $P < 0.01$). These complex biological insights
were successfully re-discovered in synthetic datasets generated by
Avatars K10 and Gaussian copula (Figure 4d).

In conclusion, the combination of metric-based evaluation and biological
replication identifies Gaussian copula as the most SDG method for
preserving DGE profile in synthetic transcriptomic data and the two
variations of Avatars shared the runner-up position where they were able
to re-discover all biological insights in original studies.

### Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Similar to the DGE evaluation, we first compared and ranked the SDG
methods using the Pathway Concordance Score (PCS). For PCS computation,
we followed a protocol analogous to GCS, where each pathway was assigned
a rank score by integrating the sign of the normalized enrichment score
(NES), reflecting regulation direction, together with the $Q$ value
(Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment for false discovery rate (FDR)
correction) to capture statistical significance. Higher PCS values
therefore indicate a greater proportion of pathways in synthetic data
that preserve both the directionality and the significance observed in
the original dataset. As in Figure 5a, scatter plots show the relation
between the original and the synthetic pathway rank scores in the
Melanoma dataset. Pathways that are in the Upper Right (UR, light green)
and Lower Left (LL, light purple) quadrants are concordant signals where
the two datasets agree on the direction of regulation and the
significance. The results for other types of cancers are included in
Figure S11-S13. According to Table 3 and Bayesian comparison heatmaps
(Figure 5c and Table S11-S13), different SDG approaches yielded several
competing best methodologies for different cancer types based on the PCS
scores: TVAE for Melanoma, Avatars K10 for ccRCC, and Gaussian Copula
for NSCLC.

Beyond global concordance, we further examined the directional
preservation of cancer-relevant pathways. Figure 5b summarizes agreement
patterns for key functional pathways in Melanoma and NSCLC. In the
Melanoma original cohort, five immune-related pathways IFN-$\gamma$
response, Allograft rejection, Complement, Inflammatory response, and
IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling, were enriched in responders, with no pathways
enriched in progressors (Figure 5a). These biological signatures were
largely recovered by Avatars (K5/K10), Gaussian Copula, Synthpop, and
TVAE, but not by CTGAN (Figure 5b). Importantly, robust recovery across
random seed replicates was only observed for Avatars K10, Gaussian
Copula, Synthpop, and TVAE. In NSCLC, both responders associated (IFN-
$\gamma$ response, Allograft rejection, DNA repair) and non-responder
associated (EMT, WNT $\beta$-catenin, TGF- $\beta$ signaling) pathways
were recapitulated with varying degrees of robustness. TVAE demonstrated
directional agreement across all these pathways in at least three out of
five replicates. While Gaussian Copula and Synthpop showed consistent
alignment with responder-associated pathways, Avatars (K5/K10) exhibited
more robust agreement across replicates for non-responder associated
pathways.

To further assess biomarker recovery, we performed GSEA comparing
9p21.3-deleted and wild-type tumors in the ccRCC cohort. In the original
data, 9p21.3 loss was associated with increased EMT, mTORC1 signaling,
angiogenesis, hypoxia, and glycolysis, alongside decreased oxidative
phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism, and TGF-$\beta$ signaling.
Synthetic datasets displayed considerable variability across replicates.
Avatars (K5/K10) and the Gaussian Copula also reproducibly recapitulated
downregulated pathways, in particular fatty acid metabolism and
oxidative phosphorylation. However, the retracing of the upregulated
pathways was less strong, with EMT being the only signal that was
consistently detected in all methods. Gaussian Copula had the best
stability for all the random seeds, including one replicate that was
almost identical to the original full pathway pattern (Figure 5d).

In the original study of Melanoma, Liu et al. reported significant
enrichment of IFN-$\gamma$ and IFN-$\alpha$ response pathways in
ipilimumab-treated responders to anti-PD1 therapy (FDR,
$Q < 0.0001\ $for both), while no such enrichment was observed in the
ipilimumab-naive subgroup (FDR, $Q = 0.13$ and $Q = 0.996$,
respectively). As illustrated in Figure 5e, Gaussian Copula successfully
reproduced this differential enrichment pattern in both interferon
pathways (IFN-$\gamma$ FDR, $Q = 0.0012\ $for Ipilimumab-treated group,
FDR, $Q = 0.997\ $*for* Ipilimumab-naive group and IFN-$\alpha$ FDR,
$Q = 0.0012\ $for Ipilimumab-treated group, FDR, $Q = 1.0\ $*for*
Ipilimumab-naive group). Avatars K10 and Synthpop partially captured
these signals, each recovering only one pathway. Moreover, Gaussian
Copula showed superior robustness across replicates, with three out of
five runs reproducing the subgroup effect, whereas Synthpop and Avatars
K10 failed to demonstrate consistent recovery.

In general, although some of the SDG approaches recovered pathway-level
signals in single simulated datasets, the level of reproducibility over
random seed replicates was quite different. Methods like Avatars
(K5/K10) and Gaussian Copula were in general more stable when retaining
biologically meaningful pathways, while other showed dramatic
variability in performance across runs. These results indicate that
single run assessments may overestimate the quality of synthetic data
and the biological relevance of transcriptomic synthetic data should be
evaluated for replicability.

### Single-sample Gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) 

Alongside GSEA, we further evaluated single-sample pathway-level
concordance by performing ssGSEA and comparing the distribution of NES
between original and synthetic datasets using the KS statistic. Across
all cohorts, Gaussian Copula achieved the highest similarity of NES
distribution to the original data, characterized by consistently high
and tightly distributed KS-Complement scores (Figure 6a). This
superiority was confirmed by Bayesian estimation, which identified the
Gaussian Copula as the optimal method for ssGSEA preservation, with
posterior probabilities exceeding 87% (Figure 6b and Table S14-S16).
Synthpop ranked second, following Gaussian Copula. Results from
additional replicates showing similar patterns are provided in Figure
S14.

Systematically, we evaluated whether known biological signals could be
recovered. In the original ccRCC cohort, loss of function PBRM1
mutations were associated with reduced IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling (Wilcoxon
rank-sum test, $P = 0.01$). As shown in Figure 5c, this signal was
robustly reproduced by the Gaussian Copula across multiple synthetic
replicates. In addition, the direction and statistical significance of
other pathways, including estrogen response, apoptosis, allograft
rejection, and UV response, were also recovered by Gaussian Copula,
whereas other SDG methods exhibited pronounced inter-replicate
variability. In the Melanoma cohort, comparison of MHC-II scores between
responders and progressors showed that only Avatars K10 and Gaussian
Copula reproduced the expected pattern that higher MHC-II scores in
responders in the ipilimumab-treated group (Mann-Whitney U (MWU) test,
$P < 0.1$) and no significant difference in the ipilimumab-naïve group
(Mann-Whitney U (MWU) test, $P > 0.1$). However, this recovery was not
robust and was observed in only a single replicate (Figure S15).

Furthermore, in the Melanoma study, the combination MHC class II score,
lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level, and lymph node metastasis status was
reported to have a strong prognostic performance for predicting
progression in ipilimumab-treated patients. To assess transferability,
models were trained exclusively on synthetic data and evaluated on
held-out folds of the original cohort using 5-fold cross-validation
repeated three times (Methods section). As illustrated in Figure 6d,
logistic regression (LR) trained on original data achieved a mean
cross-validated Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve
(ROC-AUC) of 0.82. Models trained on synthetic datasets generated by
Avatars (K5/K10), CTGAN, and Gaussian Copula achieved comparable
performance (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, $P < 0.05$). Notably, this
positive result was most robust for Gaussian Copula, with three out of
five replicates achieving statistically comparable cross-validated
ROC-AUC, whereas other SDG approaches yielded only one or two successful
replicates. To further validate these findings, LR was replaced with
multi-layer perceptron (MLP) and random forest (RF) models. The
cross-validated ROC-AUC obtained from original data decreased to 0.72
for MLP and 0.76 for RF. Interestingly, models trained on Gaussian
Copula synthetic data were still similar for both classifiers, the RF
model reached a mean ROC-AUC of 0.82 as well as the LR (Figure S16). As
well, the coefficients for all three features in the LR model were
negative. The models trained on Avatars K10 and Gaussian Copula
synthetic datasets consistently preserved the directional alignment of
coefficients across all replicates (Figure 6d). In general, Gaussian
Copula reliably attained the highest level of performance in terms of
ssGSEA distributional similarity, recovery of known biological signals
and downstream predictive modeling. Yet these findings also suggest an
important limitation: high marginal agreement at the pathway level does
not imply that the biological interpretation or predictive performance
can be reliably recovered as significant inter-replicate variability can
still arise when moving into downstream analyses.

### Cell type deconvolution

In this dimension, we examined whether cell type composition inferred
from bulk RNA-seq could be faithfully preserved in synthetic data.
Immune deconvolution was performed using CIBERSORTx with the LM22
reference signature matrix. Given the compositional nature of immune
cell fractions, similarity between original and synthetic immune
landscapes was quantified using the Aitchison distance across datasets
(Table 3).

Across all three cohorts, SDG methods showed a clear decline in
performance when moving from ccRCC to Melanoma and NSCLC. The highest
overall Aitchison similarity for ccRCC was achieved by Synthpop (0.816 ±
0.057), Gaussian Copula was a close second (0.798 ± 0.079). Avatars K10
achieved comparable results in ccRCC (0.756 ± 0.038) and displayed a
mild decrease in Melanoma and NSCLC, whereas high inter-replicate
variation was observed for Avatars K5. In contrast, TVAE and CTGAN were
found to produce consistently lower Aitchison similarity scores across
all cohorts. Pairwise Bayesian estimation also ranked Synthpop as the
best method globally, with Gaussian Copula and Avatars being second best
depending on the cohort (Figure S17 and Table S17-S19).

Beyond global concordance, we evaluated whether immune-related
biological signals could be recovered. Using CIBERSORTx-based LM22
deconvolution, differential analysis between immune-infiltrated and
immune-excluded/desert tumors in the ccRCC cohort identified enrichment
of CD8+ T cells, follicular helper T cells, activated CD4+ memory T
cells, and M1 macrophages in infiltrated tumors, whereas excluded/desert
tumors exhibited higher proportions of M2/M0 macrophages, resting CD4+
memory T cells, resting NK cells, and eosinophils (Figure 7a). Among the
SDG methods, only Avatars and Gaussian Copula managed to reconstruct
immune contrasts at a near significance level (Wilcoxon rank-sum test,
FDR, $Q < 0.25$). Of note, synthetic replicate reproducibility was
achieved only for cell types that already had very strong significance
in the original data (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, FDR, $Q < 0.05$), such as
CD8+ T cells and resting CD4+ memory T cells. For all other cell types,
which showed weaker or non-significant effects in the original cohort,
the corresponding synthetic results were not robust and varied
substantially between replicates. In Melanoma and NSCLC, differential
analyses between responders and non-responders revealed no significant
LM22 cell-type differences in the original data, consistent with prior
reports; accordingly, SDG methods did not produce stable signals in
these settings (Figure S18).

We next analyzed immune signatures derived from single-cell RNA-seq
reported by Jerby-Arnon et al.^25^ In the original Melanoma cohort, all
immune signatures were enriched in responders (Figure 7b). Most SDG
methods, except CTGAN, preserved the directionality of these signatures
at a relaxed significance level (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, FDR,
$Q < 0.25$). However, only Gaussian Copula and TVAE showed consistent
agreement for the immune signatures that were most significant in the
real data ($Q < 0.25$), such as overall immune infiltration, cytotoxic
and exhausted CD8+ T, CD4+ T, B, and macrophage cells. Notably, TVAE
frequently produced highly significant results across replicates,
alongside apparent effect-size inflation. This observation was
accompanied by extreme imbalance in responder to progressor ratios in
TVAE-generated synthetic datasets, ranging from 10.1% to 692.9%,
compared with a balanced ratio in the original cohort (83.9%).

In NSCLC, single-cell-derived immune signatures showed strong
associations between resistance and myeloid-driven immune programs,
including monocyte/macrophage and dendritic cell, myeloid subpopulations
(e.g., hMono3 and hN3) signatures.^26,27^ While the association
direction of these signatures were partially recovered in several
synthetic datasets, level of significance varied across replicates. To
further probe higher-order immune organization, we performed integrative
correlation analysis (Figure 7c). In the original data, two
well-separated clusters emerged: a wound-healing associated cluster
dominated by immunosuppressive myeloid, stromal, EMT, and TGF-β
signatures linked to resistance (C1), and an immune
activation/exhaustion cluster enriched for immune infiltration,
immunoproteasome components, and IFN-γ signaling associated with
response (C2). This structure was clearly preserved in Gaussian Copula
and Avatars K5 synthetic data, partially retained by Avatars K10, and
largely lost in Synthpop, CTGAN, and TVAE.

In summary, while most SDG methods preserve global cell type composition
structure, the recovery of specific biomarkers is highly method
dependent. Gaussian Copula provided the optimal balance between global
concordance, biological interpretability. Importantly, good performance
for marginal cell-type proportions (such as Synthpop) is not sufficient
to ensure reliable biomarker identification, highlighting the need to
preserve joint dependencies between cell type composition and clinical
outcomes. Due to the inter-replicate variability observed, multiple
synthetic realizations are advisable for biologically reliable
inference.

### Survival analysis

We evaluated whether synthetic data preserved clinically meaningful
survival patterns by comparing overall survival (OS) and
progression-free survival (PFS) between responders and non-responders
across three cancer cohorts. Survival discrimination was quantified
using the concordance index (C-index) derived from Cox proportional
hazards models fitted separately on original and synthetic datasets for
OS and PFS. To measure survival similarity between datasets, we defined
a C-index score based on the absolute difference between original and
synthetic C-indices (averaged across OS and PFS), where higher values
indicate greater agreement in discrimination performance. In parallel,
Kaplan-Meier survival differences between responder groups were assessed
using the log-rank test. Detailed score calculations are provided in the
Methods section.

As shown in Table 3, overall, two distinct performance clusters were
observed across datasets. Avatars (K5/K10) and Gaussian Copula
consistently achieved high C-index scores (\>0.9), indicating close
concordance between original and synthetic survival discrimination. In
contrast, CTGAN, Synthpop, and TVAE yielded lower scores (\<0.9),
suggesting incomplete preservation of survival-related signals. Bayesian
estimation confirmed Avatars (K5/K10) and Gaussian Copula as the most
reliable methods for maintaining survival information (Figure S20 and
Table S20-S22). Performance was stable across random seeds, as reflected
by the small standard deviations of C-index scores. In ccRCC, Avatars
(K5/K10) and Gaussian Copula preserved significant differences between
responders and non-responders for both OS (log-rank test, Avatars K5:
$P < 0.01$, Avatars K10: $P < 0.01$, Gaussian Copula: $P < 0.01$) and
PFS (log-rank test, Avatars K5: $P < 0.01$, Avatars K10: $P < 0.01$,
Gaussian Copula: $P < 0.01$). CTGAN retained significance for OS only
(log-rank test, $P = 0.0167$), whereas Synthpop preserved significance
for PFS only (log-rank test, $P = 0.0358$), indicating partial recovery
of survival signals. TVAE was excluded from this cohort because it
generated only a single treatment label (Intermediate clinical
benefit-ICB) across replicates, excluding for further survival
comparison. In Melanoma, Avatars and Gaussian Copula maintained
significant OS (log-rank test, Avatars K5: $P < 0.01$, Avatars K10:
$P < 0.01$, Gaussian Copula: $P < 0.01$) and PFS (log-rank test, Avatars
K5: $P < 0.01$, Avatars K10: $P < 0.01$, Gaussian Copula: $P < 0.01$)
separation consistent with the original data. TVAE also produced
statistically significant differences (log-rank test, OS and PFS:
$P < 0.01$); however, it markedly distorted the
responder-to-non-responder ratio (84% vs. 10% in the original cohort),
indicating label imbalance despite apparent discrimination. CTGAN and
Synthpop did not preserve significant OS or PFS differences (log-rank
test, OS and PFS: $P > 0.05$). In NSCLC, Avatars and Gaussian Copula
again retained significant separation for both OS and PFS (log-rank
test, $P < 0.01$), whereas CTGAN, Synthpop, and TVAE failed to maintain
survival discrimination for either endpoint (log-rank test, OS and PFS
$P > 0.05$). The Kaplan-Meier curves and their associated C-indices
across all three-cancer cohorts are available in Figure S21-S27 .

Consistent with other downstream task validations, we evaluated whether
synthetic datasets preserved biomarker stratification patterns
previously reported in prior studies. First, Braun et al. demonstrated
that somatic alterations modulated response to PD-1 blockade in ccRCC,
with patients harboring PBRM1 alterations exhibiting improved outcomes
compared with wild-type cases. This genotype outcome association was
faithfully recapitulated in synthetic data generated by Avatars K10 and
Gaussian Copula (Figure 8a, log-rank test, Avatars K10: $P < 0.05$,
Gaussian Copula: $P < 0.05$), indicating effective preservation of
mutation-linked survival dependencies. Second, Liu et al. developed a
parsimonious predictive model integrating MHC-II ssGSEA scores with
genomic features to stratify progressors and non-progressors, where
patients with lower predicted risk scores achieved better survival
outcomes. We re-implemented this modeling framework using classifiers
trained on synthetic datasets, evaluated predictive performance (see
ssGSEA section), and subsequently conducted survival stratification.
Notably, only the Gaussian Copula synthetic dataset consistently
reproduced this survival separation for both PFS (Figure 8b, log-rank
test, $P < 0.05$) and OS (Figure S28, log-rank test, $P < 0.05$). On the
other hand, TVAE showed preservation for PFS (Figure 8b, log-rank test,
$P < 0.01$), but it could not preserve OS stratification (Figure S28,
log-rank test, $P > 0.05$). Finally, within the NSCLC cohort, Ravi et
al. reported that among patients with high PD-L1 expression (TPS ≥ 50%),
increased macrophage/monocyte infiltration was associated with reduced
PFS despite otherwise favorable prognostic status. This clinically
relevant survival signature was again successfully recovered using
Gaussian Copula synthetic data (Figure 8c, log-rank test, $P < 0.05$),
whereas none of the other SDG methods reproduced this association.

Collectively, while Gaussian Copula and Avatars (K5/K10) effectively
preserved global survival relationships between OS, PFS, and treatment
response, only Gaussian Copula consistently retained downstream
clinically interpretable stratification patterns across multiple
oncology datasets.

## Privacy risk evaluation

In addition to being high quality, privacy of synthetic data needs to be
concerned, especially for sensitive clinical transcriptomic data. To
quantify the privacy risk, we made use of the Anonymeter framework to
assess the risks of singling out, linkability and attribute inference.
These three measures correspond closely to the main principles of
factual anonymisation as recommended by the EDPB. In terms of singling
out, we measure the risk at both the univariate and multivariate
predicate levels and ask whether predicate(s) construted from the
synthetic data can single out an individual record in the original data
set. For linkability, we measured the risk that an adversary could use
synthetic records as a bridge to reconnect disjoint sets of attributes
(e.g., clinical and molecular subsets) belonging to the same individual.
For attribute inference, we assessed the attacker's ability to
accurately infer unknown "secret" attributes, such as clinical outcomes,
using auxiliary transcriptomic information.

For ease of comparison, after simulating multiple attack scenarios, we
transformed the resulting risks into an overall privacy score, where
higher values indicate lower privacy risk and thus stronger privacy
preservation. The procedures for risk quantification and privacy score
calculation are detailed in the Methods section. Table 4 reports the
overall privacy scores for each synthetic dataset across the three
cancer types.

For univariate singling out attack, predicates were constructed in an

datasets. This behavior is consistent with Synthpop's high KS-complement
scores, indicating near identical marginal distributions between
synthetic and original data (Figure 9a). Within the Anonymeter
framework, an attack is considered failed when its success rate does not
exceed the naive random baseline. For multivariate singling out, the
number of attributes included in predicates was progressively increased.
As expected, the effectiveness of singling-out generally decreased with
higher-dimensional predicates (Figure 9b), reflecting the simultaneous
increase in baseline chance success and reduced specificity of targeted
attacks. Nevertheless, Synthpop retained the highest multivariate
singling out risk across all cohorts. Linkability risk remained
consistently low across all SDG methods and cancer types (Figure 9c),
indicating that synthetic datasets largely prevent record-level
re-identification through attribute bridging, even when progressively
larger transcriptomic feature subsets were used for linkage. Attribute
inference represented the most substantial residual privacy risk across
methods. Using transcriptomic features to infer clinical or genomic
attributes, inference risks ranged approximately from 0.4 to 0.75 across
datasets (Figure 9d). In ccRCC, Avatars K5 and K10 exhibited higher
average inference risks (0.74) compared with other approaches (0.4-0.5),
while CTGAN showed the highest inference risk in the Melanoma cohort
(0.66). In NSCLC, all methods demonstrated comparable inference risks
(0.4-0.5). Notably, numerical clinical attributes consistently displayed
lower inference risk than categorical attributes, reflecting
Anonymeter's evaluation design in which categorical secrets are assessed
via exact-match classification, whereas numerical attributes are
inferred through regression based on relative error thresholds.

Overall, aggregated privacy scores and Bayesian comparative analysis
(Figure 9e and Table S23-S25) suggest that CTGAN and Gaussian Copula
offer the best privacy preservation among tested SDG models on
oncology datasets. In contrast, the least privacy-preserving method is
Synthpop, which is largely affected by the extremely high singling out
risk (Table 4).

## Computational resources

Computational resource requirements critically shape the real-world
feasibility of synthetic data generation in biomedical settings. Table 5
reports hardware configuration, peak memory usage, execution time,

SDG method.

Across cohorts, Gaussian Copula required high CPU utilization (32 cores)
and substantial memory, particularly in the ccRCC cohort (up to \~96
GiB). However, runtimes were relatively short (1.7-15 ks), and energy
consumption remained low (\<1 kWh across cohorts). Configuration
complexity primarily stemmed from the need to properly encode
categorical variables (e.g., ordinal vs. one-hot encoding). Thus,
although Gaussian Copula demands considerable memory and preprocessing,
it remains practical on CPU-only machines with sufficient RAM. In
contrast, deep generative models (CTGAN and TVAE) incurred substantially
higher computational costs. Both required GPU acceleration (1xV100) and
long runtimes (\~3-40 hours depending on cohort size). Energy
consumption was correspondingly higher, reaching \~10 kWh in ccRCC.
Although memory usage was moderate compared to Gaussian Copula,
dependence on specialized hardware and prolonged execution time may
limit deployment in resource-constrained environments. Synthpop
exhibited a heterogeneous computational profile. While operating on
CPU-only hardware, it required very high memory (up to \~156 GiB in
ccRCC) and long runtimes (up to \~36 hours for larger cohorts). Notably,
these values were obtained under a specifically configured setup in
which predictor matrices were manually designed to constrain variable
dependencies and reduce memory burden. In a naïve default configuration
without tailored predictor matrices, resource consumption may be even
higher due to unconstrained modeling of full variable interactions.

evaluated methods, Avatars is the only proprietary tool. Jobs can be
submitted via a web interface or through the *octopize-avatar* Python
API. However, the API requires the feature space to be partitioned into
blocks, as the full data matrix cannot be processed simultaneously. This

## Meta-ranking methods

We calculated the rank-derived meta-score for each SDG method followed
the protocol of Yan and partners.^24^ These meta-scores provide a
general ranking of SDG methods integrating all metrics from 3 aspects
broad utility, narrow utility and privacy. Figures 10a and 10b present a
comparison of meta-score of SDG models, evaluated across individual
cancer types and aggregated across all three cancer datasets,
respectively. As expected, Gaussian Copula was the best-performing
method across both specific cancer types and the combined datasets,
followed by two variations of Avatars in the next positions. A
utility-privacy trade-off was identified: CTGAN obtained higher privacy
scores, whereas Avatars (K5/K10) and Gaussian Copula had lower scores,
aligning with their better performance in terms of utility.
Additionally, although Synthpop achieved a competitive meta-score in
broad utility, it ranked in the penultimate position due to poor privacy
performance regarding singling-out risk.

# Discussion

This study introduces a unified benchmarking framework to systematically
evaluate SDG methods for transcriptomic clinical data across three
oncology cohorts (ccRCC, Melanoma, and NSCLC). The results show that no
single SDG algorithm excels across all evaluation dimensions (Tables
2-4), highlighting trade-offs between statistical fidelity, downstream
biological utility, and privacy preservation. Nevertheless, Gaussian
Copula consistently emerged as a strong overall performer, achieving a
balanced performance across these dimensions and datasets (Figure 10a,
b).

A key finding is the divergence between broad utility metrics, which
assess statistical similarity, and narrow utility measured through
downstream biological tasks. For example, although Synthpop achieved the
highest univariate similarity across cancers (Table 2), it failed to
preserve higher-order feature co-dependencies, such as MHC gene
cluster-mutational burden architecture (Figure 3) and
wound-healing-immune activation programs (Figure 7). In contrast,
Gaussian Copula and Avatars maintained these complex biological
structures and rediscovered clinically relevant biomarkers, including
PBRM1-associated angiogenesis in ccRCC and immunoproteasome enrichment
in NSCLC (Figure 4). These results indicate that strong broad utility
does not ensure preservation of intricate biological relationships,
reinforcing the need for task-specific evaluation. A utility-privacy
trade-off was also evident: methods closely mirroring marginal
distributions, such as Synthpop, showed elevated singling-out risks,
whereas Gaussian Copula and CTGAN demonstrated comparatively stronger
privacy protection (Table 4; Figures 9-10). We also observed attenuation
of statistical significance in synthetic datasets. Although the
directionality of gene-, pathway-, survival-, and immune-associated
signals was often preserved, effect sizes and statistical strength were
generally more conservative than in the original data (Figures 4, 7, 8).
Thus, synthetic data appear suitable for hypothesis generation but may
underestimate subtle biological effects.

Method performance was influenced by dataset characteristics,
particularly extreme feature dimensionality and variable sample sizes
(Table 1). In ccRCC (\>40,000 transcriptomic features), all methods
showed reduced concordance in gene-wise fold change, underscoring the
difficulty of modeling ultra-high-dimensional spaces (Supplementary).
However, survival-related dependencies were consistently preserved by
Gaussian Copula and Avatars across cancer types (Table 3; Figure 8),
supporting their robustness for modeling clinical outcomes. Conversely,
deep generative models such as TVAE and CTGAN were computationally
demanding and exhibited instability in smaller cohorts, including
structural degradation and imbalance in responder-progressor ratios.

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic benchmark specifically
targeting high-dimensional transcriptomic clinical cancer data. A
central contribution is the development of a disease-agnostic,
modality-aware evaluation framework integrating three complementary
dimensions: broad utility, narrow utility, and privacy risk. This work
assesses whether current SDG approaches can address the statistical and
structural challenges of large-scale gene expression data combined with
heterogeneous clinical variables.

A distinguishing feature of this study is its emphasis on narrow
utility. We developed and ensembled task-specific metrics aligned with
common transcriptomic analyses and evaluated whether synthetic data
could rediscover clinically and molecularly relevant associations,
including gene-level signals, pathway alterations, and established
biomarkers. By grounding evaluation in downstream biological validation,
this benchmark treats biological fidelity as a core criterion rather
than a secondary outcome.

Given the computational burden reported in Table 5, we assessed
robustness by repeating the synthesis process five times per method for
each dataset. To address the limited sample size inherent to this
setting, we adopted a Bayesian framework for pairwise method comparison
instead of relying solely on Null Hypothesis Significance Testing
(NHST). With only five replicates, traditional p-value-based testing can
become overly rigid, often leading to the familiar "black-and-white"
pitfalls when the null hypothesis cannot be rejected (p \> 0.05). In
contrast, the Bayesian approach enables an uncertainty-aware comparison
by estimating the probability that one method outperforms another across
the distribution of replicates, thereby avoiding binary conclusions
while remaining appropriate for small-sample evaluation.

Regarding privacy evaluation, there is currently no consensus on
standard privacy metrics or acceptable risk thresholds for synthetic
data. Similarity-based measures (e.g., distance to real data) are
insufficient for two reasons: they do not reliably reflect inference
risk, and reporting such similarity metrics may itself facilitate
reconstruction attempts by adversaries. Moreover, overlap between
similarity and utility metrics complicates interpretation of the
utility-privacy relationship. Accordingly, we employed attack-based
evaluations using the Anonymeter framework to simulate realistic
adversarial scenarios and directly quantify singling-out, linkability,
and attribute inference risks in alignment with EDPB principles.

This benchmark further provides a standardized, reproducible end-to-end
pipeline encompassing preprocessing, transcriptomic harmonization,
multivariate imputation, model training, evaluation, and downstream
validation. The framework is designed to be reusable and extensible for
benchmarking emerging SDG methods or selecting approaches suited to
specific biological and privacy constraints.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The evaluation framework,
although multi-dimensional, does not exhaustively cover all possible
utility and privacy metrics, particularly for task-specific
applications. While diverse paradigms were included, not all SDG
approaches were evaluated. The small sample sizes typical of clinical
cohorts may have limited generative model performance and reduced
precision of privacy risk estimation. Moreover, without large external
control datasets, distinguishing population-level signal from unintended
memorization remains challenging; privacy risks should therefore be
interpreted as relative, scenario-dependent indicators.

Computational constraints required harmonized but non-exhaustive
hyperparameter exploration, and alternative configurations may alter
performance. Rank-based meta-scores, while enabling integrative
comparison, compress absolute values and may not fully reflect
downstream biological robustness; they should be interpreted alongside
absolute metrics and biological validation.

In summary, rigorous evaluation of synthetic transcriptomic clinical
data requires a multi-dimensional framework integrating statistical
fidelity, downstream biological validity, and privacy risk. We encourage
researchers to apply this framework to their own datasets, prioritize
preservation of biologically meaningful dependencies beyond metric-based
similarity, and perform task-specific validation when selecting SDG
methods. Continued refinement of biologically grounded metrics and
privacy assessment strategies will further strengthen benchmarking
efforts and support transparent, context-aware use of synthetic data in
precision oncology.

# Methods

## Datasets

To ensure the general applicability of the benchmarking framework, we
selected paired clinical and transcriptomic data from three independent
studies: Braun et al.,^3^ Liu et al.,^14^ and Ravi et al.^15^ These
three cohorts address three different cancer types: clear cell renal
cell carcinoma (ccRCC), melanoma, and non-small cell lung cancer
(NSCLC), and are focused on patients who received immune checkpoint
blockade (ICB) treatment.

Together, these data sets represent a variety of clinical settings and
transcriptomic signatures related to ICB response across tumor types. A
description of the cohort in terms of number of patients and the
transcriptomic characteristics for each dataset are given in Table 1.

## Data processing pipeline

To minimize the confounding impact of data quality on generative model
effectiveness, a standardized and reproducible preprocessing pipeline
was designed and implemented, and consistently applied across all three
datasets. This harmonized processing ensures unbiased comparability
among the evaluated synthetic data generation methods. The pipeline
consists of the following five stages:

1.  Raw Data Filtering: Quality control was initially conducted on the
    molecular and clinical data. Duplicate entries and entries without
    unique identifiers were removed, leaving a single primary record.
    For transcriptomics data, genes that were not expressed (zero
    counts) across all samples or exhibited near-zero variance (variance
    \<0.001) were excluded. Moreover, a threshold on the degree of
    missingness was employed to remove features or patients with too
    much missing data, in order to ensure enough data density for
    subsequent analyses.

2.  Transcriptomic Harmonization: The MyGeneInfo Python library was used
    to map Ensembl IDs to HUGO gene symbols.^28^ For datasets already
    reporting gene expression in HUGO symbols, this step was omitted. In
    cases of many-to-one Ensembl-to-gene mapping, expression values for
    redundant Ensembl IDs were averaged. Additionally, this step
    generated a dictionary to group genes exhibiting identical
    expression profiles across the cohorts.

3.  Feature type classification: A metadata schema was constructed for
    each input dataset, specifying the data type of every feature.
    Features were categorized as categorical, ordinal, or numerical.
    Categorical features included non-numeric variables, binary
    variables, or numeric variables with fewer than ten unique values,
    and were automatically inferred by the pipeline (e.g., sex,
    treatment arm). Ordinal features represented categorical variables
    with an inherent order but undefined distances between levels, such
    as disease stage or number of prior therapies. These were typically
    pre-encoded, but in ambiguous cases, expert clinical annotation was
    required. All remaining continuous variables, including age,
    survival metrics, and gene expression, were classified as numerical.

4.  Multivariate imputations: To improve the quality of imputation,
    categorical variables were one hot encoded, ordinal variables were
    encoded using an ordinal encoder and numerical variables were scaled
    using Min-Max scaler to speed up the model training. Missing values
    were filled in by using either Multivariate Imputation by Chained
    Equations (MICE)^29^ or K-nearest neighbors (KNN).^30^ MICE was
    preferred because it is flexible in handling the heterogeneity of
    this data modality and can accommodate missing completely at random
    (MCAR) and missing at random (MAR) mechanisms.^29^ When facing
    non-convergence or multicollinearity, MICE didn't work, so KNN was
    used as an alternative. In order to explicitly maintain patterns of
    missingness in the synthetic data, binary indicators of missingness
    were created for those features that had missing values before
    imputation. These flags indicated whether a value was originally
    observed or missing, and under the generative framework also allow
    for learning of feature-specific missingness patterns during
    training and synthesis of new data. Once the imputation was done,
    all the features were inverse transformed to the original scale in
    order to retain the biologically meaningful distributions before the
    synthetic data is generated.

5.  Data Integration: The clinical and transcriptomic data were linked
    through patient-specific identifiers to create an integrated dataset
    that could be used for subsequent synthetic data modelling and
    analysis.

A schematic overview of the data processing pipeline is provided in
Figure S1 in the supplementary materials.

## Synthetic data generation protocol

The benchmarking standard was used to assess five methods for the
generation of synthetic data: CTGAN,^16^ TVAE,^16^ Gaussian Copula,^13^
Synthpop,^17^ and Avatars^18^ across the three cancer types. The core
algorithms in Avatars method uses dimensionality reduction and k-nearest
neighbors as its main algorithms.The key settings are the number of
nearest neighbors and the number of components. We ran Avatars with k
set to 5 and 10, without changing the number of components. These
configurations are called Avatars K5 and Avatars K10.

We applied all six synthetic data generation methods to each cancer
dataset. This process was repeated five times using different random
seeds. In total, we created 90 synthetic datasets, with 30 for each
cancer type.

Naïve fitting techniques did not work because the data was
high-dimensional and heterogeneous. Instead, we used method-specific
procedures to generate synthetic data at scale:

-   *[Gaussian Copula:]{.underline}* We used one-hot encoding for
    categorical features. For ordinal categorical features, we used the
    OrdinalEncoder from Scikit-learn.^31^ To manage high-dimensional
    data, we fit univariate marginal distributions in parallel across
    multiple CPU cores using joblib. We grouped column-wise operations
    into batches with a vectorized chunking approach to reduce
    communication overhead. When inverting, we mapped one-hot encoded
    variables back to their original categories by picking the category
    with the highest probability. For ordinal variables, we rounded
    synthetic values to match the original labels.

-   *[Avatars:]{.underline}* Avatars is a proprietary tool developed by
    Octopize that requires a license. We used the official Python client
    to upload the original data to the API and receive the synthetic
    data. Because of memory limits with large, high-dimensional
    datasets, we could not upload the full dataset at once. Based on
    advice from the Avatars technical team, we split each dataset into
    blocks of fewer than 4,000 records. We anonymized these blocks one
    at a time using the API, then combined the synthetic blocks to
    create a single synthetic dataset. To partition features, we
    measured pairwise associations. We used Spearman's rank correlation
    for numerical features and Cramér's V for categorical features. For
    associations between numerical and categorical features, we divided
    numerical variables into ten bins and treated them as categorical.
    We then created a distance matrix from the correlation matrix as
    follows:

$$distance\ matrix = 1 - \ |correlation\ matrix|$$

A lower distance value means a stronger association between a pair of
features. Hierarchical clustering with average linkage and the distance
matrix to cluster features. Heatmaps of the clustered distance matrices
for two cancer types are shown in the Supplementary.

-   [Synthpop]{.underline}: Synthpop generates synthetic data by
    iterating through a predefined sequence of variables, where each new
    variable is synthesized using a conditional predictive model trained
    on the previously generated variables in the sequence. If the
    default configuration is used, the order aligns with the input data
    and, each new variable is synthesized using all previously generated
    features. As the synthesis moves further down the sequence, the
    number of predictors increases in an arithmetic progression, since
    each model incorporates all previously synthesized variables as
    inputs, leading to the memory issue and significant increase in
    computation time. To miltigate it, we designed the predictor matrix
    to define the synthesis order, importantly, the number of predictors
    to model each feature.

Particularly, pairwise associations were computed by the framework
applied to Avatars (Spearman for numerical features and Cramér's V for
categorical features). The correlation matrix was transformed into a
distance matrix $d_{ij} = 1 - \left| \rho_{ij} \right|$, where
$\rho_{ij}$ denotes the association between feature $i$ and $j$. A
binary adjacency matrix was then constructed by thresholding this
distance matrix ($d_{ij} \leq 0.7$). To further constrain computational
resources, the number of candidate predictors for each feature was
capped at 500 by retaining the strongest associations.

The resulting binary matrix was intepreted as an undirected graph, where
nodes represent features edges correspond to pairwise associations. To
arrange the synthesis order, we ranked features using a two-criteria
centrality strategy based on degree centrality (primary criteria) and
eigenvector centrality (second criteria in the case of ties). The final
predictor matrix was constructed as a lower-triangular structure,
allowing each feature to depend only on highly correlated features that
appear earlier in the sequence. This ordering encourages influential
variables to be generated first, allowing downstream variables to
condition on the most informative predictors while preventing cumulative
predictor expansion in high-dimensional settings.

## Data utility evaluation

### Broad utility

We measured the broad utility of synthetic data by looking at two main
statistical properties: how similar the distributions of individual
features are, and how well the relationships between pairs of features
are preserved.

#### Univariate similarity

-   For numerical features: the similarity was assessed using the KS
    statistic. The simiarity score for a numerical feature $k$ is
    defined as:

$$s_{i} = 1 - KS(CDF(F_{k}^{original}),\ CDF(F_{k}^{synthetic}))\ $$

-   For categorical features: the Total Variation Distance (TVD) was
    used to quantify the similarity. The score for a categorical feature
    $k$ is defined as:

$$s_{j} = 1 - TVD(F_{k,\ \ original},\ F_{k,synthetic})$$

-   These scores were calculated by using the Synthetic Data Vault (SDV)
    library. ^32^

Then, to have a balanced evaluation between the two data modalities
(clinical and transcriptomic), the final univariate score for Method A
is the average of the mean score of each modality:

$${UnivariateScore}_{A} = \frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i \in Clinical}^{N}s_{i} + \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j \in Transcriptomic}^{M}s_{j} \right)$$

Where $N$ and $M$ denote the total number of clinical features and
genes, respectively. $s_{j}$ and $s_{i}$ are the similarity scores
calculated via KS or TVD, depending on the nature of the specific
feature.

#### Bivariate similarity 

To evaluate how well the synthetic data preseives relationships between
features, we calculated bivariate similarity score ${(s}_{pair})$ based
on the feature types:

-   For numerical-numerical pairs: we used Spearman's rank correlation
    $(\rho)$. The similarity score for a pair $(i,\ j)$ is:

$$s_{i,j}\  = \ 1 - \frac{1}{2}\left| \rho_{i,j}^{original} - \rho_{i,j}^{synthetic} \right|$$

-   For categorical-categorical and mixed (numerical-categorical) pairs:
    Cramér's V was used. For mixed pairs, numerical features were
    discretized into ten bins. The similarity score for a pair $(i,\ j)$
    is:

$$s_{i,j}\  = \ 1 - \left| V_{i,j}^{original} - V_{i,j}^{synthetic} \right|$$

To get the overall bivariate similarity score Method A, we calculated
the mean score for each of the three functional modalities and then
averaged them to ensure equal weighting:

$${BivariateScore}_{A} = \ \frac{1}{3}\left( {\overline{S}}_{transcriptomic - transcriptomic} + {\overline{S}}_{clinical - transcriptomic} + {\overline{S}}_{clinical - clinical} \right)$$

$$T = \ C_{N}^{2} = \frac{N(N - 1)}{2}$$

Where

-   ${\overline{S}}_{modality}$ is the average similarity score of all
    pairs within that category:

    -   Clinical-Clinical:
        ${\overline{S}}_{clinical - clinical} = \frac{1}{T_{cc}}\sum_{}^{}s_{i,j}$
        where $T_{cc} = \begin{pmatrix}
        N \\
        2 \\
        \end{pmatrix}$

    -   Transcriptomic-Transcriptomic:
        ${\overline{S}}_{transcriptomic - transcriptomic} = \frac{1}{T_{tt}}\sum_{}^{}s_{i,j}$
        where $T_{tt} = \begin{pmatrix}
        M \\
        2 \\
        \end{pmatrix}$

    -   Clinical-Transcriptomic:
        ${\overline{S}}_{clinical - transcriptomic} = \frac{1}{T_{ct}}\sum_{}^{}s_{i,j}$
        where $T_{ct} = N \times M$

-   $N$ and $M$ denote the total number of clinical features and genes,
    respectively

### Narrow utility

In this evaluation dimension, we assessed whether synthetic
transcriptomic data preserved important clinical and biological signals
by comparing results from several downstream analyses on both the
original and synthetic datasets. These analyses included DGE, GSEA,
ssGSEA, cell type deconvolution, survival analysis, and machine
learning-based modeling. For each task, we ran the analysis separately
on the original and synthetic data, then measured how closely the
results matched.

We defined clinical subgroups for each cancer based on specific clinical
parameters, usually splitting patients into responders and
non-responders or progressors, following the original study definitions.
For the ccRCC cohort, responders were defined as patients who achieved
an objective response (complete or partial response), as well as those
with stable disease accompanied by tumor shrinkage and a
progression-free survival (PFS) of at least 6 months. These patients
were collectively labeled as having clinical benefit (CB). Conversely,
non-responders were defined as patients exhibiting progressive disease
with a PFS of less than 3 months, labeled as non-clinical benefit (NCB).
Importantly, only patients treated exclusively with nivolumab were
included in the ccRCC analysis. In the melanoma cohorts, responders were
defined as patients achieving a complete response (CR) or partial
response (PR) as their best response according to RECIST version 1.1.
Progressors were defined as patients whose best response was progressive
disease (PD) under the same RECIST criteria. For the NSCLC cohort,
responders were defined using the same criteria as in melanoma (CR or
PR), whereas non-responders included patients with stable disease (SD)
or progressive disease (PD), in accordance with RECIST version 1.1. We
also included other subgroups from the original publications, like
genomic subtypes or prior therapy groups. The next sections describe the
procedures for each analysis task.

#### Differential gene expression (DGE)

To evaluate synthetic data in DGE task, we introduced the Gene
Concordance Score (GCS). This metric quantifies the agreement in gene or
pathways significance and directionality between original and synthetic
datasets. We calculated GCS in three steps:

-   Step 1: Rank Score Calculation: each gene $i$ is assigned a rank
    score ($RS$) that combines its efffect size and statistical
    significance:

$${RS}_{i} = sign(\log_{2}{FC_{i}) \times \left( - \log_{10}{(q - value_{i}}) \right)}$$

The scatter plots comapring gene $RS$ between the original data and the
synthetic datasets generated by each SDG method across all three cancer
cohorts are provided in Figure S4-S6 in the Supplementary Data.

-   Step 2: Concordance Zone Identification: a significance boundary is
    defined as:

$$s_{thr} = - \log_{10}(0.05)$$

Using this threshold, four concordance zones are defiend in the
$RS_{original}$ versus $RS_{synthetic}$ plane:

-   Significant Zones: Genes appearing in the same extreme quadrants
    (both significantly up-regulated or both significantly
    down-regulated in both datasets). Let $N_{sig}$ be the count of
    genes in these zones.

-   Non-signifcant Zones: Genes that remain non-significant and maintain
    the same direction of $Log_{2}FC$ in both datasets. Let
    $N_{non - sig}$ be the count of genesin these zones.

-   Step 3: GCS Calculation: the score is calculated by this formula:

$$GCS = \frac{N_{sig} + \omega.N_{non_{sig}}}{M}$$

$$M = N_{sig}^{original} + \ \omega.N_{non - sig}^{original}$$

Where $N_{sig}^{original}$ and $N_{non - sig}^{original}$ are computed
from the full set of genes in the original dataset.

We set a weight $\omega = 0.5$ to assign lower weight to non-significant
genes, thereby prioritizing preservation of biologically meaningful
differential signals.

#### Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

We implemented GSEA with the GSEAPy Python package. Pre-ranked GSEA was
performed with 10,000 gene set permutations to calculate enrichment $P$
values, FDR $Q$ values and NES for each pathway. We analyzed all three
cancer cohorts using the Hallmark Gene Sets from the Molecular
Signatures Database (MSigDB.

To measure how well the original and synthetic data matched, we employed
the Pathway Concordance Score (PCS). Analogous to GCS, PCS evaluates the
agreement in enrichment significance and directionality at the pathway
level. For each pathway $p$, a rank score ($RS_{p}$) was defined as:

$${RS}_{p} = sign(NES) \times ( - \log_{10}{(FDR - q_{p}}))$$

Concordance was subsequently assessed within the same signed
significance framework and weighting scheme described for GCS.

#### Single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA)

We performed ssGSEA using GSEAPy with the log-rank method and the
Hallmark gene sets from MSigDB. To assess preservation of pathway-level
activation signals at the single-sample level, we quantified the
similarity between the distributions of NES in the original and
synthetic datasets.

For each pathway $p$, similarity was measured using the KS-complement
score:

$$KSComplement_{p} = 1 - KS(CDF\left( {NES}_{p}^{original} \right),\ CDF({NES}_{p}^{synthetic}))$$

The overall performance for a given method was defined as the mean KS
complement score across all 50 Hallmark pathways.

#### Predictive modeling

To assess if the synthetic data preserved the predictive utility of the
original data, we compared the performance of classification models on
synthetic datasets against those trained on the original dataset. The
model was used to distinguish progressors from non-progressors within
the Ipilimumab-treated subgroup of the melanoma cohort. Three
classifiers including logistic regression (applied in the original study
by Liu et al), random forest, and multilayer perceptron, were evaluated
using repreated stratified cross-validation (5 folds, 3 repeats,
*RepeatedStratifiedKFold* fucntion). The computations were implemented
by scikit-learn Python library.^33^

For each cross-validation split, models were trained either on the
original training set or on the corresponding synthetic training set,
while performance was consistently evaluated on the same held-out test
folds from the original data. This design ensured that all comparisons
were conducted on identical real-data test distributions. Model
performance was quantified using the ROC-AUC metric.

To statistically compare predictive performance between models trained
on original versus synthetic data, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were
applied across cross-validation folds. A non-significant difference
combined with comparable or improved mean performance was interpreted as
evidence that the synthetic data preserved predictive utility.

#### Immune cell decovolution

We used the CIBERSORTx algorithm for cell deconvolution to match bulk
transcriptomic data. The LM22 signature estimated the fractions of 22
immune cell types. We ran the analyses in absolute mode with B-mode
batch correction, turned off quantile normalization, and performed 1,000
permutations. We excluded samples with CIBERSORTx deconvolution
$P > 0.05$. This procedure was consistently applied across all three
cancer cohorts.

To quantify preservation of inferred immune cell compositions, we
measured the similarity using the Aitchison distance, which is
appropriate for compositional data.^34^

Let $x_{ij}$ denote the estimated fraction of cell type $j$ in sample
$i$. After contraining to the top $k\ (k = 10)$ most abundant cell types
(identied from the original data), zero values were handle using
multiplicative replacement.

The compositional center (geometric mean) for each cell type $j$ was
computed as:

$$g_{j} = exp\left( \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i = 1}^{n}{\log x_{ij}} \right)$$

The centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation of the compositional center
vector $g_{j}$ is:

$$CLR\left( g_{j} \right) = log\left( \frac{g_{j}}{\left( \prod_{l = 1}^{k}g_{l} \right)^{1/k}} \right)$$

The Aitchison distance between original and synthetic datasets is then
defined as the Euclidean distance in CLR space:

$$d_{A} = \sqrt{{\sum_{j = 1}^{k}\left( CLR\left( g_{j}^{original} \right) - CLR\left( g_{j}^{synthetic} \right) \right)}^{2}}$$

To ease of comparison, the distance was converted into the Aitchision
distance score:

$$Score = exp( - d_{A})$$

where higher value indicate the better compositional concordance.

Next, we did differential analysis on the immune cell proportion
matrices. For ccRCC, we used the original study design and CD8
immunofluorescence assays to classify tumors as immune-infiltrated,
immune-desert, or immune-excluded, and compared these groups directly.
For melanoma and NSCLC, we compared responders and non-responders or
progressors.

#### Survival analysis

To quantify the survival similarity between the original and synthetic
datasets, the C-index was first estimated independently for each dataset
using a Cox proportional hazards model from either OS or PFS, as
implemented in the *lifeline* Python package. Then, the final C-index
score was defined as:

$$C_{score}^{OS} = 1 - \left| C_{original}^{OS} - C_{synthetic}^{OS} \right|$$

$$C_{score}^{PFS} = 1 - \left| C_{original}^{PFS} - C_{synthetic}^{PFS} \right|$$

$$C_{score}^{final} = \frac{C_{score}^{OS} + C_{score}^{PFS}}{2}$$

Where $C_{original}^{OS},\ C_{synthetic}^{OS}$ denote the C-index of the
original and synthetic datasets based on OS;
$C_{original}^{PFS},\ C_{synthetic}^{PFS}$ denote the C-index of the
original and synthetic datasets based on PFS.

In terms of Kaplan-Meier curve analysis, $P$ value of a log-rank test
was calculated to compare survial curves of two observed groups.

## Privacy evaluation

According to EDPB, there are three criteria to assess the anonymization
methods. They are singling-out, linkablity and inference:

-   Singling out is the ability to isolate a specific individual within
    a dataset.

-   Linkability is the ability to connect two records belonged to the
    same patient.

-   Inference is the ability to deduce, with significant probability,
    the value of an individual using the anonymized dataset.

In our benchmarking study, we performed attak-based evaluations via the
Annonymeter framework.^35^ This framework enables us to simulate attack
scenarios and evaluate directly singling out, linkablity and inference
risk, which is coherent and aligned with EDPB.

Utilizing the Anonymeter, we designed an privacy assessment adapted to
our data modality, which are described clearly below.

### Singling out risk

Anonymeter evaluates singling out risk by attempting to isolate
individual records within the original dataset using unique predicates
derived from patterns in the synthetic data. There are two algorithms:
univariate PredicateFromAttribute and the multivariate
MultivariatePredicate, to generate singling-out predicates. The
univariate algorithm generates predicates from single features, while
the multivariate configuration leverages attribute combinations to
identify individuals within the dataset.

To evaluate univariate singling-out attacks, we tested different
configurations by randomly sampling 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of the
dataset\'s attributes. For each of these configurations, Anonymeter
generated 10,000 predicates (as defined by the $n_{attacks}$ parameter)
to simulate adversary attempts.

For multivariate singling-out attacks, attackers were provided with
combinations of multiple attributes, with the number of attributes per
attack set to $\left\{ 2,3,5,7,10,20,50 \right\}$. Risk estimation
followed the same randomized attack protocol as in the univariate
setting where 10,000 predicates were generated.

#### Linkability

Anonymeter evaluates linkability risk by determining if an attacker can
use a synthetic dataset to connect disjoint sets of attributes, referred
to as auxiliary information, belonging to the same individual. The
framework simulates this by identifying the a nearest neighbor in the
synthetic data for each attribute set independently, utilizing the Gower
coefficient to measure similarity across both categorical and numerical
data. That means the input for Annonymeter in linkability assessment is
the 2 disjoint sets of feature. In our experiment, we want to valiate
the risk that an attacker can leverage synthetic datasets to connect
molecular profile to clinical informations of a patient. Therefore, 2
disjoint sets are clinical features and transcriptomic features. We
iteratively picked up 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% genes in gene expression
matrix to link all clinical variables.

#### Attribute inference risk 

This risk was quantified by assessing an adversary's ability to infer
sensitive attributes (secrets) from auxiliary gene expression features.
Similar to linkability, Anonymeter utilized the k-nearest neighbors
algorithm to predict secret attributes in the original data based on
synthetic samples. For inference attacks, each clinical variable was
treated as a potential secret, and risks were computed independently for
each target attribute.

#### Overall privacy score

In the Anonymeter framework, beside the main attack risk using synthetic
data ($R_{attack}$), the naïve risk ($R_{naive}$), corresponding to a
baseline obtained under random guessing, was also reported. According to
the authors, if the main attack risk is lower than or equal to the
random naïve baseline ($R_{attack}\  \leq$ $R_{naive}$), the attack is
classified as "failed" and should not be reported as "zero risk".
Therefore, we reported failed attacks following their suggestion (Figure
9). In the overall privacy score calculation, for each attack
configuration $c$, we used naïve risk ($R_{naive}$) instead of using 0
of the attack failed, as

$$R(c) = max(R_{attack}(c),R_{naive}(c))$$

For each privacy category $m$, the final risk for a given synthetic data
generation method was obtained by averaging over all configurations:

$${\overline{R}}_{m} = \frac{1}{\left| C_{m} \right|}\sum_{c \in C_{m}}^{}{R_{m}(c)}$$

Where $C_{m}$ denotes the set of attack configuration for category $m$.

To obtain an overall privacy risk, we aggregated the four privacy
categories, including univariate singling-out, multivariate
singling-out, linkability, and attribute inference, using an unweighted
mean:

$$R_{overall\ } = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{m = 1}^{4}{\overline{R}}_{m}$$

Finally, we report an overall privacy score defined as

$$Privacy\ Score = 1 - R_{overall}$$

where higher values indicate stronger privacy preservation. All privacy
evaluations were repeated across five random seeds to account for
stochastic variability in synthetic data generation.

## Rank-dervied meta-score

We adapted the ranking framework of Yan et al.^24^ to intergrate 8
evalution aspects: univariate similarity, bivariate similarity, DGE,
GSEA, ssGSEA, dell deconvolution, survival analysis, and privacy.

In the context of each cancer dataset, 6 SDG methods (5 replicates each;
30 candidates) were ranked per aspect. The rank-score of method $m$
under aspect $a$ was defined as the average rank across its five
replicates:

$$RankScore_{m,a} = \frac{1}{R}\sum_{r = 1}^{R}{Rank_{m,r,a}}\ (R = 5)$$

Evaluation aspecs were grouped into:

-   Broad utility: univariate, bivariate similarity

-   Narrow utility: DGE, GSEA, ssGSEA, cell deconvolution, survival
    analysis

-   Privacy

The final meta-score within one cancer dataset was:

$$MetaScore_{m} = \frac{1}{3}\left( {\overline{RankScore}}_{m,broad} + {\overline{RankScore}}_{m,narrow} + {RankScore}_{m,privacy} \right)$$

Where each dimension contributes equally

As for cross-cancer comparison (three cancer types), we summed the
dimension-level rank-derive scores across cancers:

$$MetaScore_{m}^{all} = \frac{1}{3}\left( \sum_{c}^{}{\overline{RankScore}}_{m,broad}^{(c)} + \sum_{c}^{}{\overline{RankScore}}_{m,narrow}^{(c)} + \sum_{c}^{}{RankScore}_{m,privacy}^{(c)} \right)$$

## Statistical tests and Bayesian comparison

All statistical tests were reported alongside the corresponding results
in the main text. We used *scipy.stats* python package to perform
hypothesis testing.^36^ To compare numerical features between patient
categories (e.g., DGE, differential analysis of ssGSEA score, cell type
signatures), two-sided non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test (*ranksums*
function) or Mann-Whitney U test (*mannwhitneyu* function) were applied,
depending on the test used in the original studies. In machine learning
model comparison, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was employed (*wilcoxon*
function). Multiple hypothesis testing was controlled using the
Benjamini-Hochberg procedure for FDR correction (*multipletests*
function from the *statsmodels* python package).^37^

We performed pairwise Bayesian comparisons following the framework
proposed by Benavoli et al., using the *baycomp* python library. For
each cancer cohort, all SDG methods were compared pairwise based on
their performance scores across five independent replicates. A Bayesian
correlated t-test was applied to estimate posterior probabilities for
three mutually exclusive hypotheses:

-   Better probability ($P(SDG_{1} > SDG_{2})$): the probability that
    $SDG_{1}$ outperforms $SDG_{2}$.

-   Worse probability ($P(SDG_{1} < SDG_{2})$): the probability that
    $SDG_{1}$ underperforms $SDG_{2}$.

-   Pratical equivalent probability: the probability that the
    performance difference lies within a predefined Region of Practical
    Equivalence (ROPE).

The ROPE threshold was set to 0.01, below which performance differences
were considered negligible. The resulting "better" probabilities were
visualized as $N \times N$ heatmaps, where each cell represents
$P(row > column)$, defined as the posterior probability that the method
in the row outperforms the method in the column for the corresponding
cohort.
